Week 1 Online Moduels

About this week's

This week introduces the foundations of systems thinking and why it matters in project management. We’ll explore what it means to view a project as a system – and as something delivered within a broader system – and distinguish between clear, complicated, and complex systems. You’ll also be introduced to different types of project complexity and risks which systems thinking can help us tackle. In class, we’ll use the Triangle Game to experience system behaviour firsthand and work together to map the components and risks of a real-world project system.   

Learning Objectives

After completing this module, you will be able to: 

• Define what a “system” is and explain how projects function as and within systems 

• Distinguish between clear, complicated, and complex systems 

• Describe the five types of project complexity using the Remington & Pollack framework (including relational complexity) 

• Classify different types of project risk based on the level of uncertainty 

Note of Week 1 Slides:

What is a System?

• Definition: Systems are sets of interrelated components that interact (sometimes in seemingly mysterious ways) to achieve a common goal, where:

Each essential part can affect the behavior or properties of the whole

No essential part can have an independent effect on the whole

The system is defined by the interactions of the parts within it, not by the performance of parts taken separately

• Etymology: "system" < Greek systemi, "I place together”

• A system must be considered as a whole — predicting its behaviour demands holistic thinking

• A system is not a set: e.g. a furniture flat pack is a set of components; it only functions as a system once assembled

The Importance of Projects

• Global economy increasingly driven by project-based work; project-oriented industries forecast to contribute US$20.2 trillion to global GDP over the next 20 years

• PM has evolved from a tool-oriented approach into a holistic, competence-based management discipline

• ~11.4% of project spend wasted due to poor performance — US$114M per US$1B invested; ~US$1M wasted every 20 seconds; ~US$2 trillion per year (PMI 2020)

Modern organisations are: Interconnected + Interdependent + Rapidly evolving

3. Three Paradigms for Leading Complex Projects (ICCPM 2018)

In sum: Every complex project can and should be understood as an asset AND a system AND a conversation. This unit focuses primarily on the System aspect. Applies to complex projects of any size.

4. Types of Systems

• Examples: human musculo-skeletal system = Clear; building an airplane = Complicated

• Complicated and complex overlap significantly in projects — a complicated project easily becomes complex

• A complex project can undergo a phase transition into chaos

Pesticide Case (System A vs B)

• System A (assumed): pesticide → insect population declines → crops flourish = Clear

• System B (actual): pesticide kills insects AND their natural predators → insect population explodes → crops destroyed = Complicated

• Lesson: the observer mistook a complicated system for a clear one — a very great trap in project management!

• Deepening understanding of a complicated system over time can make it become more clear

System of Systems

• Human body = nervous + cardiovascular + musculoskeletal + digestive + reproductive systems

• Project ⊂ program/portfolio ⊂ organisation ⊂ local socio-cultural system ⊂ global socio-cultural system

5. History of Systems Thinking

The Enlightenment — Reductionism

• Descartes (1596–1650): reductionist approach — understand an object by analysing its individual parts

• Also mechanistic (living things as machines) and materialistic

• "Positivism" (Ulrich 1994): the elevation of empirical-analytical science to a guarantor of meaningful knowledge

• In projects: WBS decomposition as the positivistic approach — grossly inadequate, because interrelationships and interdependencies must also be understood

The Early Biologists

• Late 18th century: limits of reductionism became evident → holistic/organismic approach proved more useful

• 1830s: Müller, Schleiden, Schwann — cell theory demonstrated the internal relations of living things

• 1859: Darwin's Origin of Species; Whitehead: the organism is "a unit of emergent value”

The Twentieth Century

• Traditional science solved organised simplicity; statistical mechanics (late 19th c.) handled unorganised complexity (e.g. gases)

• But most real-world problems lie in between: organised complexity — the systems region

Example: 2014 Ebola Outbreak

• Deterministic/statistical models proved inadequate; disease transmission = organised complexity → needs systems-based modelling

• Psychological & sociocultural factors organised the system: fear-induced contact-rate reduction; increased hygiene; indigenous burial protocols; fear of dying in quarantine; fleeing the region; uprisings

• → Human and social factors must be considered in approaching complexity ("messiness") in projects

General Systems Theory (GST)

• Post-WWII activity in general systems theory, cybernetics, complexity theory

• von Bertalanffy, General System Theory (1968)

• GST addresses systems with strong nonlinear interactions: "the whole is greater than the sum of its parts”

• Rejects reductionism (biology purely in terms of physics) and the "robot model" of humans → humans as an "active personality system", inner-related, creating their own universe

• Project relevance: "Human behavior... is potentially a significant source of complexity in projects, and an effective antidote to it" (Cooke-Davies 2011)

Chaos Theory

• Lorenz (1972): sensitivity to initial conditions = the butterfly effect ("Does the flap of a butterfly's wings in Brazil set off a tornado in Texas?”)

• Non-linear relationships make prediction impossible in complex natural & social systems

• Yet Lorenz found hidden order: weather settled around patterns → strange attractors

• Langton: edge of chaos — a narrow "phase transition space" between order and chaos where novelty and evolution emerge

• Stacey (1996) applied to organisations (a project is a temporary organisation): (1) stable state → ossify; (2) unstable state → disintegrate; (3) edge of chaos / bounded instability → full potential for inventiveness and innovation

• Between order and disorder a "hidden order" can appear: behaviour never repeats exactly but is drawn to strange attractors

Complex Adaptive Systems (CAS)

• Chaos theory covers physical systems with fixed elements; complexity theory also embraces CAS — agents capable of learning and adapting as they interact

• Definition (Mitchell 2009): "a system in which large networks of components with no central control and simple rules of operation give rise to complex collective behavior, sophisticated information processing, and adaptation via learning or evolution”

Caution in Management Contexts

• Not all nonlinear dynamical systems exhibit complex behaviour

• Social systems: humans have self-consciousness and free will, embrace facts and values, self-reflect, learn, adapt → cannot be explained like natural systems

• Restricted complexity: explainable with traditional tools (nonlinear equations, few simple rules) — e.g. starling flocking

• General complexity: human actors themselves are complex; mutual interaction between micro (people) and macro (political-cultural) levels, both with causal power

6. Typologies of Complex Projects

Remington & Pollack (2016) — used in this course

• Structural: high number of interrelated tasks and activities

• Technical: radical innovation

• Directional: lack of clarity about goals and direction

• Temporal: changes in the project environment over time

• Extended with Relational: tangled human relationships and power dynamics that shape project outcomes

• Key point: except in directionally complex projects, uncertainty is a consequence of complexity; in directional projects, uncertainty about goals produces the complexity

Complexities in Combination

• The bigger the project/program, the more likely it exhibits all types in varying degrees

• Example (global telecoms HR-process project): Structural = program size; Technical = new IT system & integration; Directional = departments lack shared understanding of objectives; Temporal = volatile legislation changing over time; Relational = tensions/trust issues between teams in different countries and head office vs local HR managers

7. Risk & Uncertainty

"There are known knowns... known unknowns... But there are also unknown unknowns — the ones we don't know we don't know." — Donald Rumsfeld

Black Swan events (Taleb)

Outlier — outside regular expectations; nothing in the past points to its possibility (an unknown unknown)

Extreme impact

Retrospective explainability — human nature concocts explanations after the fact, making it seem explainable and predictable

• Positive Black Swans: occur more slowly (e.g. the internet)

• Negative Black Swans: dramatic, rapid onset (e.g. 9/11, Japanese tsunami, 2008 GFC, COVID-19 — all debatable)

• Origin: Juvenal's "a bird on earth as rare as the black swan" (rara avis); popularised by Nassim Nicholas Taleb

Key Takeaways

• A system = interrelated components interacting toward a common goal; must be viewed holistically (not a set)

• A project is an asset + a system + a conversation; this course focuses on the system view

• System types: Clear (known knowns) → Complicated (known unknowns) → Complex (unknown unknowns) → Chaotic; misjudging the type is a major PM trap

• Reductionism (Enlightenment) cannot handle organised complexity → systems thinking needed

• Systems thinking history: reductionism → cell theory/Darwin → GST (whole > sum of parts) → chaos theory (butterfly effect, edge of chaos, strange attractors) → CAS (no central control, simple rules, adaptation)

• Human behaviour is both a source of project complexity and an antidote to it

• Five complexity dimensions: structural / technical / directional / temporal / relational

• Four risk categories: known knowns / known unknowns / unknown unknowns (Black Swans) / unknown knowns

Note of Chapter 1 of Tools for Complex Projects

1. Core Argument

• Nearly all large (and many small) projects exhibit complexity, yet they're still managed with linear thinking rooted in PM traditions dating to the Egyptian pyramids and early-modern control theories from industrialisation.

• Control-based approaches only work when outcomes can be predetermined and delivered as planned — i.e. small, short projects. Beyond a critical size/timeframe/ambiguity/interconnectedness, they fail.

• Projects are systems, but most writing uses "hard systems thinking" (Checkland) — fine for mechanical systems, not useful when people are the key elements.

• People are self-determining, self-willed, selfish, unpredictable — they can disrupt the best-laid plans simply by refusing to play.

• High project failure rates (Remington & Crawford 2004; Williams 1999; Baccarini 1996) show control-only methods are inadequate → PMs need a systemic AND pluralistic approach.

2. Systemic Pluralism

• Required to deliver success in complex contexts:

Recognise the systemic nature of projects

Adopt a pluralist approach to tools and theories

• Developed under Critical Systems Thinking (Midgley; Mingers; Flood & Jackson).

• Book draws on Complexity Theory (cross-disciplinary science of CAS: emergence, non-linearity, sensitivity to initial conditions; applied to management by Stacey 1996, Griffin et al. 1999).

• Most projects are better described as complex adaptive systems than simple systems.

• No one size fits all — managers need a palette of tools, selecting per situation. A single project can show multiple kinds of systemicity.

• Some projects/parts are effectively simple (well-defined outcomes → traditional tools very efficient); other parts need complex systems thinking.

3. Aim of the Book

• A guide, not a prescriptive methodology — a "bag of tools" selected by informed judgement.

• Focus on tools NOT normally in the PM's palette. Traditional tools remain valid when objectives are clear, understood, agreed, stable over time.

4. A Complex Project is a Complex Adaptive System

• All projects exhibit: interconnectedness, hierarchy, communication, control, emergence.

• Most large projects ALSO exhibit: phase transition, adaptiveness, sensitivity to initial conditions.

• Systems depend on viewpoint: no repeatable pattern = single occurrence, not a system. (Dirk Gently / pile of apples example.)

8 CAS Characteristics

Hierarchy — nested sub-systems (babushka dolls): chemist → team → department → organisation → industry; WBS is the formal depiction.

Communication — info passed between elements and across boundaries (grapevine); rapidity ≠ accuracy (Chinese whispers); formal vs informal comms.

Control — holds the system together (negative feedback): body thermostat; group norms enforced by jokes → escalation to exclusion.

Emergence — properties appear only at higher levels (the whole > sum of parts): a bicycle's "ridability" exists only at whole-bicycle level.

Phase Transition — same system takes new form in response to changing conditions: Descartes' melting wax; navy vessel moving to battle stations.

Non-linearity — driven by positive feedback (induces change; contrast = control/negative feedback): The Beatles after Ringo joined — creative spiral.

Adaptiveness — system adjusts its reference points to survive/improve vs environment: vitamin producers adapting to new regulatory standards.

Sensitivity to Initial Conditions — the butterfly effect (Lorenz 1972): same team/project in a different environment → radically different performance; small anomalies can escalate risks out of control.

5. Four Types of Project Complexity (Remington & Pollack)

• Source of complexity → influences life cycle, governance, resources, scheduling, budgeting, risk, procurement, contracts.

6. Complexity in Combination

• Bigger project/programme → more likely to exhibit all four types.

• Example: global telecoms HR-process change — Structural (programme size) + Technical (new IT integration) + Directional (departments lack shared understanding) + Temporal (volatile legislation).

• Not all parts are complex: standard sub-projects were managed fine with standard PM tools → systemic pluralism in practice (view whole as system of sub-systems; match approach to each part).

7. More Useful Concepts

Order → Chaos Continuum

• Fully ordered systems: not complex (rigid, can't adapt). Chaotic systems: not complex either (no stable relationships, no internal coherence, don't react as a whole).

• All complex systems exist between order and chaos. Closer to order = more efficient but less adaptive; closer to chaos = more open/creative but risk losing coherence.

• Renaissance geometric city plans vs mediaeval organic growth — architectural order ≠ social order.

Fitness Landscapes

• Fitness = height (view). You climb to a local peak and stay — reaching a higher peak means crossing valleys of lower fitness (time, risk, not always worthwhile).

• Current peak may be only "good enough" — seen in design projects where delivery urgency outweighs perfecting the design.

• Landscape is not static (moving sea / shifting dunes) — it changes over time AND with your own movement; a local peak can become a valley → take many viewpoints.

Edge of Chaos

• Poised state between order and chaos: max creativity + sensitivity to environment while retaining coherence to survive.

• Location varies with landscape ruggedness: flat → control & efficiency; rugged → wider boundaries, exploration.

• Well-defined project → edge of chaos close to order (decomposition, specialisation work). Turbulent project → edge moves toward chaos (try multiple options, no specialisation yet).

8. Chapter Summary

Systemic pluralism — many perspectives, many tools, match to situation

Four types of complexity — each with distinct management challenges

Combination — large projects show multiple types; simple parts use standard PM

Order–chaos continuum — neither fully ordered nor fully chaotic is complex

Fitness landscapes — moving peaks/valleys; higher isn't always worth it

Edge of chaos — creativity + enough order to survive

Note of Video 1

Core definition

• Systems thinking = understanding how systems are interconnected + understanding the dynamics within systems

• A system = a configuration of parts connected and joined together by a web of relationships or networks

• Systems thinking is nonlinear: cause and effect are not necessarily linked by simple step-by-step chains

• Connections between human and natural systems are of particular interest because they offer excellent examples of cascading effects — what seems to be a simple outcome of a system can actually produce a series of effects on other interconnected systems

The hot dog example

• Producing just one condiment — mustard — involves a global network: mustard seeds from India, fancy mustards prepared in France, turmeric from India, paprika from Hungary or the Netherlands, plus salt, water and white vinegar

• The system behind a single condiment is already complex — let alone a fully loaded hot dog

Why systems thinking is hard

• People are not well equipped for systems thinking; it's much easier to understand simple linear cause-and-effect chains

• The more complex the system dynamics, the harder it is to know how decisions will play out

• Decisions or actions at one point often impact another component within the system (e.g. warmer weather → mustard seed production decreases → price rises → production further limited, mustard becomes more expensive/less available)

Key principles

Systems thinking does not claim complete knowledge — it's about assessing the degree of system complexity, analysing system dynamics, and making decisions that reduce the risk of negative outcomes

See the interconnected nature of all elements; reacting to a problem in one part of the system may have unintended consequences on other components or the process as a whole

How to develop systems thinking

• Explore how things might change under different circumstances

• Look at possible associations and connections beyond the information being presented

• Conduct an institutional analysis — a robust review of existing practices, mechanisms and procedures in place

• Share findings with relevant stakeholders and discuss both hidden and visible flows

• Seek possible explanations and relationships

• Think more globally about how you are part of major economic, environmental and social systems — how these impact one another and the immediate systems you work within

Note of Video 2

1. Core Idea

Systems thinking = thinking about your thinking.

Information makes you informed. Thinking makes you smart. The goal is to become aware of the patterns your brain already uses to build meaning, so you can do it better.

Information: Raw data from senses, reading, observation

Thinking: The process of organizing information and giving it meaning

Knowledge: The result of thinking — what you build from information

Systems Thinking: Being aware of how you think and how the world works

2. Mental Models

Mental model = the knowledge structure you build when you make meaning from information, memories, and experiences.

Key takeaways:

• Everyone builds mental models, from birth, without needing language.

• Your mental models are unique to you — shaped by your unique experiences.

• Mental models determine how you feel and how you act.

• Your mental model of a situation (e.g. a dog) directly shapes your behavior (pet it or not).

"Lots of information makes us informed, but lots of thinking makes us smart. There are just some things you cannot Google.”

3. The Four Patterns (DSRP)

All mental models are built using these four patterns, simultaneously:

3.1 Distinctions — Identity ↔ Other

Every thing has an identity because there is an "other" — everything it is not.

Identity: What a thing is (you, coffee, the color red, happiness, the USA)

Other: What a thing is not (everything else)

• We make distinctions with all our senses.

• Your distinctions are different from others' — and that makes life interesting.

Self-check: Pick an object near you. What is it? What is it not?

3.2 Systems — Part ↔ Whole

We organize things into systems of parts and wholes. Every whole is made of parts; every part can itself be a whole with its own parts.

Part: A component of a system

Whole: The complete system made of interacting parts

Example: Toilet + Sink → each is a whole made of parts; together they are parts of a larger water system → all water on Earth is one planetary system.

• Zoom in → see the parts.

• Zoom out → see the whole.

• Systems give us a framework to understand the world.

Self-check: What system are you a part of right now? What are the parts of that system?

3.3 Relationships — Action ↔ Reaction / Cause ↔ Effect

A relationship is an interaction between ideas or objects — not just between people.

Action / Cause: What one thing does to another

Reaction / Effect: What happens as a result

• Relationships exist at all scales: atomic particles to galaxies.

• Seeing a new relationship (or an old one in a new way) can spark a new idea.

• Understanding interconnections helps you solve problems that matter.

Self-check: Pick two seemingly unrelated things around you. Is there a hidden relationship between them?

3.4 Perspectives — Point ↔ View

Every perspective has a point (where you're looking from) and a view (what you see).

Point: The position/lens you're looking from

View: What you see from that position

• You already use perspectives constantly (e.g. navigating streets with spatial awareness).

• You can adopt perspectives of other people, nations, or ideas.

• "Perspective determines not just what we look at, but what we really see.”

Perspective underpins: empathy, compassion, connectedness — how we relate to others and the world.

Self-check: Think of a disagreement you've had. What was the other person's point? What was their view?

7. Practice Prompts

Sample Answer of Workshop Activities for Week 1

Activity 2A — Parramatta Light Rail (PLR) as a System

Evidence per complexity type:

• Structural: The project involves hundreds of moving parts — multiple government agencies (Transport for NSW, Sydney Water, City of Parramatta Council, Sydney Olympic Park Authority), a main works contractor (John Holland, $322M enabling works contract), utility owners whose trunk mains must be relocated (two Sydney Water pipelines in Ermington), and a peak construction workforce of 750–1,000 people across multiple sites.

• Technical: Innovative long-span, cast-in-situ balanced cantilever bridge design (only two piers in the river to reduce environmental footprint); integration of new light rail alignment with active transport links, existing roads and utilities; relocation of critical trunk water mains — which can only be done in winter and one pipeline at a time.

• Directional: Political disagreement over the project's purpose — the former Liberal government promised Stage 2 more than a dozen times without starting construction; the Minns Labor government delivered it as a Labor election commitment. Communities and councils differ on priorities (housing, transport access, public space vs fast delivery).

• Temporal: Construction spans 5–6 years (2025–2030/31); legislative/planning approvals changed hands (approval secured 2024); the environment (population growth, housing policy in Western Sydney) evolves while the project is built.

• Relational: Success depends on relationships between state and federal governments, Council, contractors, and the community along the corridor (Wentworth Point, Melrose Park, Olympic Park); ongoing community engagement is built into the delivery strategy.

Dominant complexity: Structural — While the project shows all five types, its defining management challenge is coordinating an enormous number of interconnected tasks, agencies, contractors and dependencies across multiple phases (Enabling Works, 2a, 2b). The political/directional friction is real but secondary to the sheer scale of organisational coordination required, which is why it was delivered through an Early Contractor Involvement (ECI) Design and Construct contract — a classic structural-complexity response.

Activity 2B — Classify the AI Project

Evidence per type:

• Structural: Many moving parts — data pipelines, model training, integration into existing systems, staff review workflows, multiple specialist teams.

• Technical: Genuinely uncertain whether the approach will work — model performance on the organisation's own data is unknown; tools are evolving mid-build.

• Directional: Nobody fully agrees what "success" means — cost saving, new capability, or being seen to innovate? The board wants it live this year; who decides?

• Temporal: The technology and the regulations around it keep changing while the project is being built.

• Relational: Staff review the model's output — their trust (or fear) in AI and relationships between IT, operations and leadership decide whether it actually succeeds in use.

Dominant complexity: Directional — The project's core difficulty is not building the AI; it is that the organisation has no shared definition of success (cost saving vs new capability vs being seen to innovate), and the board's "live this year" deadline pre-empts the time needed to align stakeholders. In a directionally complex project, the uncertainty about goals produces the complexity itself — the technology could be straightforward if everyone agreed on what they were trying to achieve.

Discussion question — Is the AI hard to build, or hard to agree on? It is primarily hard to agree on. A well-defined AI task in a team that shares its goal is fairly straightforward; the same technology in an organisation that disagrees on purpose becomes complex on every dimension. The dominant complexity type changes with the project: PLR is dominated by structural complexity (physical scale), whereas the AI project is dominated by directional complexity (goal ambiguity) — confirming that what makes a project complex is often the setting, not the technology.

Optional Activity — Risk & Complexity Typology

Columbia Shuttle Disaster

• Complex or complicated? The physical system was complicated (identifiable cause-and-effect chains — foam strike → damaged thermal protection → re-entry failure). But the organisational system in which it operated was complex: many interacting agents (NASA culture, hierarchical decision-making, fragmented risk communication) whose interactions could not be predicted, and which failed to adapt to early warning signs.

• Risk type: The foam strike itself was a known unknown (the issue had occurred on prior flights). The full failure chain, however, behaved like an unknown unknown — the severity of the interaction was not anticipated, so warning signs were downplayed.

• Features that made outcomes hard to anticipate: organisational culture that downplayed past anomalies; hierarchical decision-making suppressing dissent; fragmented communication across specialist teams; sensitivity to initial conditions (the launch-day foam strike).

Climate Change

• Complex or complicated? Clearly complex — too many interacting agents (governments, industries, communities) with nonlinear feedbacks; outcomes cannot be predicted from any single part. It is a genuine complex adaptive system, not a complicated one.

• Risk type: Largely unknown unknowns — the timing and magnitude of tipping points and system responses cannot be anticipated; also conflicting risk definitions among stakeholders make even known knowns contested.

• Features that made outcomes hard to anticipate: deep interconnectedness; nonlinearity (human inputs and feedbacks); conflicting stakeholder priorities and risk definitions; political/cultural/economic complexity impeding coherent action despite strong scientific consensus.

Wrap-up Reflection (sample)

• One system I'm part of this week: The university itself — a system of timetables, Canvas, lectures and student groups that keeps functioning even though no single person is in charge of the whole thing.

• What stuck with me: That a system can be "complex" while each of its parts is simple — the complexity lives in the interactions, and misjudging the type of system is the biggest trap in project management.

• Where systems thinking helps everyday life: When planning anything multi-person (a group assignment, a move), mapping the dependencies and feedback loops first beats trying to control every detail.
